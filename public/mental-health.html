Mental Health and Parkinson's

Written by Bryce Perry

Founder of Doing Life Today • Parkinson's Advocate • Host of Living with Parkinson's: The Good, the Bad, and the Reality podcast

Updated March 2026

Author Bio

Parkinson's affects more than movement. Understanding the emotional side of the condition can help people manage anxiety, stress, and mood changes.

When most people think about Parkinson's, they focus on the physical symptoms.

Tremor. Stiffness. Slowed movement.

But Parkinson's also affects the emotional side of life.

Many people living with Parkinson's experience anxiety, depression, stress, or changes in mood. These experiences are incredibly common, yet they are often not talked about enough.

Understanding the emotional impact of Parkinson's is an important part of managing the condition and maintaining overall wellbeing.

If you are recently diagnosed and looking for a broad overview of what to expect, our Newly Diagnosed guide is a good starting point. For more on the full range of non-motor symptoms, the Parkinson's Symptoms guide covers this in depth.

Anxiety and Parkinson's

Anxiety is one of the most common emotional symptoms reported by people living with Parkinson's.

It can appear in several ways.

  • Feeling constantly on edge
  • Worrying about symptoms
  • Feeling overwhelmed by uncertainty
  • Physical sensations such as a racing heart or tight chest

Sometimes anxiety is connected to medication timing or fluctuations in dopamine levels.

Other times it can be related to the uncertainty that comes with a chronic condition.

Recognizing anxiety early can help people find strategies that make it easier to manage.

Depression and Mood Changes

Depression is another common experience for people living with Parkinson's.

It does not always look like sadness.

Some people experience:

  • Low motivation
  • Loss of interest in activities
  • Persistent fatigue
  • Feelings of hopelessness

Parkinson's affects brain chemistry, which means mood changes can sometimes be part of the condition itself and not only a response to the diagnosis.

Talking openly about these changes with your doctor is important, because treatments and support are available.

Stress and Emotional Adjustment

A Parkinson's diagnosis can create stress in many areas of life.

  • Concerns about health
  • Changes in work or daily routines
  • Fear about the future

Learning to manage stress becomes an important part of living well with Parkinson's.

Some people find support through counseling, meditation, support groups, or exercise. Finding healthy ways to manage stress can improve both emotional and physical wellbeing.

Poor sleep can also increase stress and anxiety. If sleep is a challenge for you, the Sleep and Fatigue guide explores strategies that may help improve rest.

The Importance of Community

One of the most powerful tools for emotional health is connection.

Talking with others who understand Parkinson's can reduce feelings of isolation and help people feel supported.

Many people find comfort in sharing experiences, learning from others, and realizing they are not facing the journey alone.

Community support can play an important role in maintaining emotional resilience. You can find that kind of connection through the Doing Life Today Community and Support hub and inside The Club.

Movement and Mental Health

Exercise supports more than physical symptoms.

Movement also improves mood.

Physical activity releases chemicals in the brain that help reduce anxiety and support emotional balance.

Many people living with Parkinson's find that regular movement helps them feel more in control of their day. Our Exercise and Movement guide covers how to get started safely and consistently.

When to Seek Support

If emotional symptoms begin interfering with daily life, it is important to seek support. If you are supporting a loved one with Parkinson's, the Care Partners guide addresses the emotional side of caregiving as well.

Talking with a healthcare professional can help identify helpful strategies.

Support may include:

  • Counseling
  • Medication adjustments
  • Support groups
  • Lifestyle changes

Seeking support is a sign of strength, not weakness.
